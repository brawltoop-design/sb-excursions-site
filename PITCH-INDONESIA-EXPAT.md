# Черновик статьи

## What Nusa Penida's Viewpoints Don't Tell You

*Альтернативные заголовки:*
*«The Island Where the Beaches Are Cliffs»*
*«Reading Nusa Penida's Coast Before You Get to the Water»*

---

On 15 October 2025, a 32-year-old visitor went into the water at Kelingking
Beach on the west coast of Nusa Penida, at around one in the afternoon.
She had walked past the warning signs to get there. Bali's search and rescue
agency logged the call at 13:40 and sent five personnel from the Nusa Penida
post. She did not come back.

What makes that death worth writing about is not that it was rare. It is that
it was the same death that happens on that coast every year, for the same
reason, and the reason is not carelessness. It is a misreading of what the
place is.

### The strait explains the coast

Nusa Penida sits on the edge of the Lombok Strait, one of the deep-water
channels that separates the Indonesian shelf from the ocean beyond it. Cold,
nutrient-heavy water pushes through it year-round. That current is the reason
manta rays cruise the island's cleaning stations in every month of the
calendar rather than in a season, and it is the single best argument for
crossing the strait at all.

It is also the reason the coastline behaves the way it does. The same water
that feeds the mantas arrives at the island as swell with nothing in front of
it — no fringing reef, no shallow shelf, no lagoon. It lands directly on rock.

This is the fact that the photographs cannot carry. A cliff base with a strip
of white sand at the bottom of it photographs as a beach. It is not one.

### Three places, and what each of them actually is

**Kelingking** is a viewpoint. The ridge drops roughly 200 metres to the sand,
and the trail down is over 400 steep steps that take most people 30 to 40
minutes in each direction — longer coming up, in full sun, with no shade and
no water for sale at the bottom. The beach at the base carries an official
no-swimming rule. It is widely ignored, and the October death is what ignoring
it looks like.

**Angel's Billabong** is a tidal shelf under a rock overhang, and it is
probably the most dangerous place a tourist can casually stand in the Bali
area. At least four people have drowned there. In 2018 a 46-year-old Chinese
visitor was taking a photograph at the edge when a wave crossed the shelf and
took him and his friend out; the friend was recovered, he was not. In October
2024 a 59-year-old Indian visitor was swept from the same precipice while his
wife survived with bruises.

The mechanism is always the same, and it is worth understanding rather than
just fearing. At low tide the pool sits still and green and looks like an
infinity bath. The sets that fill it do not arrive gradually. One wave crosses
the shelf, and the water that drains back off it takes whatever is standing
there. There is no swimming out of it and nothing to hold.

**Broken Beach**, the collapsed sea cave next door, has no water access at all.
It is a viewpoint and only ever was one — which, on this coast, is a feature.

The island's one straightforward swim is **Crystal Bay**, on the west side,
and it fills with day-tour traffic from late morning.

### There is no hospital

Nusa Penida has small local clinics. It does not have a full hospital.
Anything beyond cuts, stings and dehydration means a boat back to the
mainland, on a channel that is not always crossable at short notice.

That one fact should shape the whole day. It is not the place to push through
dizziness halfway up the Kelingking steps, or to attempt a swim you would call
marginal at home, or to take a scooter onto roads that are steep, narrow and
broken in places, on a licence you do not actually hold.

### The day runs on a timetable nobody publishes

The crossing from Sanur takes 30 to 45 minutes depending on conditions, and
boats land at Toya Pakeh or Banjar Nyuh on the north-west coast. The strait is
the fast part. The island roads are not: Kelingking looks close on a map and
is a real drive on steep concrete.

Two times govern everything. The viewpoints fill from late morning, so the
difference between the first boat and the third one is the difference between
photographing Kelingking and queuing for it. And the last scheduled fast boat
back to Sanur is 17:00, with one operator listing 17:15 and nothing after —
but the honest number is 16:30, because that is when the real exodus sails and
when the queue at the harbour stops moving. People who plan a sunset on Penida
without booking a bed on the island are planning to miss the boat.

### What the day looks like when it goes right

Nothing in the above is an argument against going. Kelingking and Broken Beach
have no equivalent on mainland Bali, and neither do the mantas.

But the island rewards being read correctly rather than being improvised on.
Take the first boat. See the west viewpoints before the coaches. Treat Angel's
Billabong as a place you look at from above and do not climb down to. Swim at
Crystal Bay, early, before the traffic. Keep the return boat, not the sunset,
as the fixed point of the day. And accept that Penida is spectacular
infrastructure-poor terrain, not a resort island that happens to be offshore.

The people who get hurt there are almost never reckless. They are people who
thought they were going to a beach.
